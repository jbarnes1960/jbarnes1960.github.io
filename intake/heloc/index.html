HELOC or HELOAN?

A HELOC (home equity line of credit) is a revolving line secured by your home: the rate is usually variable (often Prime-based), there is a draw period (often interest-only) followed by repayment, and you borrow only what you need, when you need it. A HELOAN (home equity loan) pays out as a single lump sum at closing with a fixed rate and fixed monthly payments over a set term. Either one is typically a second lien, so your existing first mortgage stays in place. Rates, terms and eligibility vary by borrower and lender.

Who each tends to fit: a HELOC for ongoing or uncertain costs, such as renovations done in phases; a HELOAN for a one-time, known amount, such as debt consolidation, where a predictable payment matters.

Not sure yet? Here is how each one works

Both products let you borrow against the equity in your home, usually as a second loan behind your current mortgage. The main difference is how you receive the money and how the payment behaves. HELOC: a line of credit you draw from

How it works. You are approved for a credit limit. During the draw period (often several years) you can borrow, repay and borrow again, up to that limit. Many HELOCs let you make interest-only payments while you draw. When the draw period ends, repayment begins and you pay down the balance over a set number of years. The rate is usually variable.

Ways people use it:

  • Renovations paid in phases as contractors invoice you
  • An emergency reserve you only use if you need it
  • Tuition paid semester by semester
  • Bridging the gap between buying a new home and selling the old one
  • Business or investment cash flow, where the program allows it

Possible advantages

  • Borrow only what you need, when you need it
  • You pay interest only on the amount you have drawn
  • Interest-only payments are often available during the draw period
  • Repay and reuse the line while the draw period is open
  • Closing costs can be low or none, depending on the program

Things to weigh

  • A variable rate can rise, so your payment can change
  • The payment can jump when the draw period ends and repayment begins
  • Easy access to funds can tempt you to borrow more than planned
  • In some conditions the lender can freeze or reduce the line

HELOAN: a fixed loan paid out all at once

How it works. You borrow a set amount and receive all of it at closing. You repay it with equal monthly payments over a set term, typically at a fixed rate, so the payment and the payoff date are known from day one.

Ways people use it:

  • Consolidating higher-rate debt into one payment
  • A one-time project with a known bid or quote
  • A large purchase with a known price
  • Paying off a higher-rate loan

Possible advantages

  • Fixed rate and a predictable monthly payment
  • You get the full amount at once
  • A clear payoff schedule keeps repayment disciplined

Things to weigh

  • You pay interest on the full amount from day one, even if you do not spend it all right away
  • You cannot borrow it again once repaid, so a new need means a new loan
  • Closing costs may be higher than on some HELOCs
  • Less flexible if your costs change after closing

Three simple examples

Example only

(a) Kitchen and bath remodel, about $80,000

Leans HELOC

The work happens in three stages over about nine months: roughly $30,000, then $30,000, then $20,000. With a HELOC you draw each amount as the invoice comes due, so interest is charged on about $30,000 at first, then about $60,000, and only reaches $80,000 at the end. The trade-off: the rate is usually variable, so the payment can move as rates and your balance change.

Example only

(b) Credit cards totaling about $60,000

Leans HELOAN

The full amount is needed on day one to pay off the cards. A HELOAN pays them off at closing and replaces several payments with one fixed payment and a set payoff date. The trade-off: your home now secures this debt, and the plan only helps if the cards are not run back up.

Example only

(c) A roof now, plus possible future costs

A closer call

You know the roof costs about $40,000 and you want it done now, but you may also face other costs later, such as a repair or a family expense. A fixed HELOAN gives a predictable payment on the $40,000 but no room for later costs. A HELOC lets you draw the $40,000 now and keep the rest of the line available, but the payment can change. The deciding factor is whether certainty or flexibility matters more to you.

These examples use round numbers for illustration only. They are not offers, quotes or advice, and your situation may differ.

Quick decision questions

  • Do you know the exact amount you need?A known, one-time amount points toward a HELOAN. An amount that is a range or may change points toward a HELOC.
  • Will you need the money over time rather than all at once?Spread-out costs often fit a HELOC.
  • Do you want the same payment every month?A steady, fixed payment is the main strength of a HELOAN.
  • How comfortable are you with your payment changing if rates move?If that would be stressful, a fixed-rate HELOAN may feel safer. If you are comfortable with it, a HELOC’s flexibility may be appealing.
