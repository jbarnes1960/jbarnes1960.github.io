This week in malware

Welcome to Virus Watch Weekly #8. Research for this issue was pulled from public reporting on Monday, Nov 2, 2026 (America/Denver). This week's theme: criminals putting AI to work, and hitting the places we keep our backups. A new password stealer hands everything it takes to an AI chatbot to rank who is worth robbing, fake ChatGPT and Claude desktop apps on GitHub turned PCs into crypto-mining machines, and home and office network storage boxes from Synology are being locked by ransomware. Plus a Japanese cloud-desktop provider told customers to unplug their computers, a hidden back door that takes orders through Microsoft 365 calendar entries, and an infamous password stealer that came back from a police takedown as a full remote-control tool.

New password stealer asks an AI chatbot to rank its victims by how much they're worth

High
VOLTA-AUTODOXER (self-deleting Windows infostealer)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

This is a program that, once it gets onto a Windows PC, grabs saved passwords, saved credit cards, email, Wi-Fi passwords and crypto wallets. Then it does something new: it searches the victim's Gmail for Amazon, DoorDash and PayPal receipts to work out their home address and habits, and sends the whole profile to an AI chatbot to rank what is worth stealing first. Afterward it deletes itself, so you may never see it. The best defense is the boring one: don't run programs from unknown sources, and don't let your browser be the only place your passwords and cards live.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Only install software from the maker's official website or app store; skip 'free' cracked tools, game cheats and emulators.
  • Use a dedicated password manager instead of saving passwords and credit cards in your web browser.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in (MFA) for Gmail, Microsoft, PayPal and Amazon so a stolen password alone isn't enough.
  • If a PC may be infected, change important passwords from a different, clean device and sign out all other sessions.
  • Watch bank and card statements for small test charges, and set up purchase alerts with your card issuer.

Cisco Talos documented VOLTA-AUTODOXER, a self-deleting Windows infostealer built for resale (six build-time slots for Telegram channel, API keys and settings). It steals browser passwords, cookies and saved cards (bypassing Chrome App-Bound Encryption by launching Chrome headless), Outlook/Thunderbird profiles, Microsoft OAuth tokens, Discord and Roblox sessions, plaintext Wi-Fi keys, SSH/cloud configs and screenshots, and tries to crack crypto-wallet vaults inline. It then enriches the haul live (Gmail receipt searches for Amazon/DoorDash/Uber Eats/Grubhub/Walmart/PayPal, delivery order histories, Discord profile), builds Identity_Report.txt/JSON, and POSTs it via OpenRouter to Claude Opus 4.6 (temperature 0.2) with a 'forensic intelligence analyst' prompt; on refusal it falls back to Kimi K2, then Gemini 2.5 Flash. Output (AI_Analysis.txt) and the dossier are exfiltrated as volta_dox.zip over Telegram; without an API key it skips the AI step.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Delivered as a custom build by each criminal customer; Talos did not tie it to a single lure. Runs once, steals, then deletes itself.
Source: Cisco Talos research via Tom's Hardware (Nov 1, 2026)

Ransomware is locking Synology network storage boxes through an unpatched security hole

High
C3RB3R (Cerber variant) — '.L0CK3D' files, 'read-me3.txt' note
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

Lots of homes and small offices use a Synology 'NAS' box, a small file server that holds family photos, business documents and backups. Since about Oct 28, criminals have been breaking into ones that are reachable from the internet and scrambling every file, then asking for about $3,000 in bitcoin (doubling after five days). A password or two-step login doesn't stop this one, because the attackers use a flaw that skips the login entirely. If you own a Synology box, update it today and make sure it isn't open to the whole internet.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Update Synology DSM to 7.4-90075, 7.3.2-86009-4, 7.2.2-72806-9 or 7.2.1-69057-12 (or newer). Check the full build number, not just '7.2.2'.
  • Don't leave the NAS admin page open to the internet: turn off router port forwarding and UPnP for it, and use a VPN for remote access.
  • Keep at least one backup copy somewhere the NAS can't reach, like an unplugged USB drive or a separate cloud backup.
  • Turn on DSM automatic updates and Btrfs snapshots so you can roll files back.
  • If files end in .L0CK3D, unplug the NAS from the network and power it off, then get professional help before paying anything.

Since ~Oct 28, 2026, South Korean users have reported Synology NAS devices encrypted with a Cerber variant ('C3RB3R'), appending .L0CK3D and dropping read-me3.txt demanding 0.03696 BTC (~$2,952), doubling after five days. Chinese firm 360 attributes initial access to two unauthenticated DSM flaws disclosed Sep 18 (CVE-2026-13639, predictable session entropy CWE-331; CVE-2026-13684, SCGI output encoding CWE-116; both CVSS 9.8), while Synology points to the Dec 2024 fix (Synology-SA-24:20). Common factor: DSM management exposed to the internet via port forwarding, UPnP, QuickConnect or reverse proxy. Per-file 256-bit keys wrapped with RSA-2048; no free decryptor. Fixed DSM builds: 7.4-90075, 7.3.2-86009-4, 7.2.2-72806-9, 7.2.1-69057-12 or later. JPCERT/CC flagged the flaws Sep 30; Synology Japan urged updates Oct 29.

Platforms: Synology NAS (DiskStation Manager / DSM)
How it spreads: Remote exploitation of internet-exposed DSM management interfaces; no user action or login required.
Source: Bitcomme, summarizing Korean reporting, 360's analysis and Synology's advisory (Nov 1, 2026)

Fake ChatGPT and Claude desktop apps on GitHub secretly turn PCs into crypto miners

Med
Yoba campaign: YobaDropper + YobaLoader delivering XMRig miner
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

Criminals posted fake download pages on GitHub for made-up AI apps with exciting names like 'GPT-6 Astra' and 'Claude Opus' desktop. The download includes a real, signed OpenAI or Anthropic program, so it looks trustworthy, but a hidden file tucked next to it gets loaded too. That hidden file quietly uses your computer to mine cryptocurrency for the criminals, which slows your PC, runs up your power bill and wears out the hardware. Only get AI apps from the company's own website.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Download ChatGPT only from openai.com and Claude only from claude.ai or anthropic.com, never from a random GitHub page.
  • Be suspicious of AI apps with names that don't exist yet (like 'GPT-6'); that's a common lure.
  • If your PC's fan runs loud and it's slow when idle, open Task Manager and look for unknown programs using lots of CPU.
  • Keep Microsoft Defender or your antivirus turned on and up to date.

Check Point Research's 'Yoba' campaign uses fake GitHub repositories posing as desktop apps (GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus). ZIPs ship a legitimate signed OpenAI Codex or Claude executable renamed Setup.exe alongside a malicious winmm.dll (YobaDropper) that is DLL-sideloaded and only runs under the expected host name (hash check 0xE6C2F326). YobaLoader, sideloaded by wmpdmc.exe, pulls an image from a Telegram channel, uses its last 50 bytes to seed a DGA (adjective-noun-verb-digit.dedyn[.]io), resolves a Base64 TXT record via Google DNS-over-HTTPS, decrypts a catbox[.]moe URL and injects an XMRig miner into svchost.exe. First repo created ~July 2026; malicious releases from late August.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Fake GitHub repositories and releases advertising unofficial AI desktop apps.
Source: Check Point Research via The Hacker News (Oct 29, 2026)

Japan's GMO tells cloud-desktop customers to unplug their PCs after hackers threaten ransomware

High
Onamae Desktop Cloud breach with planted ransomware threat
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

GMO runs a popular rented-computer service in Japan, often used by people who trade currencies with MetaTrader software. Hackers broke in on Oct 20, may have planted malware on up to 26,739 customer machines, demanded about $50 million, and threatened to set off ransomware. GMO refused to pay, told customers to shut down and unplug their computers, and confirmed stolen customer data was posted online. The lesson for everyone: when a service you rent gets hacked, change those passwords and follow the company's official instructions quickly.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • If you use a hosted or cloud desktop, change the passwords for every account you used on it, especially trading and banking logins.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for brokerage and MetaTrader accounts where your broker supports it.
  • Follow only the provider's official website or email for instructions; scammers often pose as the company after a breach.
  • Keep your own backup of important files and settings instead of relying on the provider alone.

GMO Internet detected an intrusion on Oct 20, 2026 into Onamae Desktop Cloud (a Japanese VPS service) that could have installed malware on up to 26,739 customer servers; it shut servers down and said payment data was not exposed but MetaTrader 4/5 credentials and trading-account details may be. The attacker demanded ¥8 billion (~$50M), which GMO refused, offering ¥5,000 per customer. On Oct 26 GMO warned of a planned leak and ransomware trigger; on Oct 27 it told customers to disconnect PCs, shut them down and unplug cables before noon while working with Trend Micro. GMO later confirmed company and customer data were published; BleepingComputer reports no ransomware actually triggered on customer machines.

Platforms: Hosted Windows VPS (Onamae Desktop Cloud); MetaTrader 4/5 trading accounts
How it spreads: Intrusion into the provider's infrastructure, with the attacker claiming malware staged on customer servers.
Source: BleepingComputer (Oct 27, 2026)

GraphFleet back door takes orders through hidden Microsoft 365 calendar entries

Med
GraphFleet (64-bit Windows backdoor abusing Microsoft Graph)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

Most malware has to 'phone home' to a criminal's server, which security tools can spot. GraphFleet instead logs in to a Microsoft 365 account and reads its instructions from hidden notes attached to a calendar event, and if that fails, from email drafts, To Do tasks or a OneDrive file. To your company's network it looks like normal Microsoft Teams traffic. It mainly matters to businesses: watch for odd Microsoft sign-ins and have your IT person know about it.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Businesses: review Microsoft 365 sign-in logs for Teams sign-ins from unfamiliar devices or locations.
  • If anything looks off, revoke the affected user's sessions and refresh tokens, not just the password.
  • Require MFA and limit which apps can get access to company Microsoft 365 data.
  • Have your IT provider check PCs for a hidden file at %LOCALAPPDATA%\Microsoft\WindowsUpdate\state.dat and a 'OneDriveSync.cmd' in the Startup folder.

Rubrik Zero Labs (Oct 30) analyzed GraphFleet, a 64-bit Windows backdoor (campaign marker gcx-fleet-2026-10, mutex Global\gc-7f2a91c4) that uses an OAuth refresh token to sign in to Microsoft Graph as Microsoft Teams (FOCI family). Primary C2 is open extensions (com.c2.cmd / com.c2.out) on a per-host 'Sync-<id>' calendar event, with fallbacks to Outlook Drafts (cmd-/out-), Microsoft To Do (notes-), and OneDrive approot settings.dat. Tokens are cached DPAPI-encrypted at %LOCALAPPDATA%\Microsoft\WindowsUpdate\state.dat; recovery via a SharePoint token drop (heightcontrollawnandlandsca-my[.]sharepoint[.]com) and an HMAC-verified BNB Smart Chain contract. Commands include shell execution, in-memory PE/shellcode loading, a script VM, self-update, and persistence via Run key, UserInitMprLogonScript, Startup folder, or a print-processor component (mpf64).

Platforms: Windows; Microsoft 365 accounts
How it spreads: Initial access not described; relies on a stolen or attacker-controlled Microsoft 365 refresh token.
Source: Rubrik Zero Labs (Oct 30, 2026)

StealC, a password stealer police shut down in June, is back as a full remote-control tool

Med
Stadia RAT (StealC rebrand)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

In June, international police shut down StealC, a widely sold password-stealing program. Four months later, its seller is back with 'Stadia,' which still steals passwords, cookies and crypto wallets but now also lets criminals secretly see and control your screen, run commands and move files. It hides under names that look like real Windows or Google updaters. It's sold to other criminals for $300 to $900 a month, so expect it to show up through all the usual traps: fake downloads, cracked software and booby-trapped links.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Avoid cracked software, game cheats and 'free' paid apps; they're the most common way stealers arrive.
  • Keep Microsoft Defender on, and treat any unknown Defender exclusion you didn't add as a red flag.
  • Store passwords in a password manager and turn on two-step sign-in for email, banking and crypto accounts.
  • If you suspect infection, disconnect the PC and change passwords from a different device.

Varonis Threat Labs (Oct 22; covered by The Hacker News Oct 29) found StealC's seller relaunched on Oct 16 as Stadia, a Windows RAT priced $300/$550/$900 per month with a free Pro month for former StealC customers. Modules: hidden VNC, remote cmd, loader (EXE/PS/CMD/MSI, disk or memory), file explorer, process manager, and a stealer built on StealC v3.5 (a byte-identical 26 KB Chromium App-Bound Encryption helper blob). Installs to C:\ProgramData\<12 random chars>\ using names like MicrosoftEdgeUpdate.exe or GoogleUpdate.exe, adds a hidden COM-registered scheduled task every 15 minutes for 3,650 days, and when elevated adds a Defender exclusion. C2 is HTTP POST of Base64 RC4 ciphertext labeled application/json; exits on Russian/CIS locales.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Malware-as-a-service; each buyer chooses delivery (typically fake downloads, cracks and loaders).
Source: Varonis Threat Labs (Oct 22, 2026)

Quick Hits

  • Binance says a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop led to the theft of about $6.3 billion in crypto on Oct 29, which TRM Labs and Chainalysis tie to North Korea's TraderTraitor (Lazarus) group; Binance says user funds are covered (BleepingComputer, Oct 30).
  • The n0n ransomware gang left its own staging server open, exposing 829 attack scripts, five encryptor versions and an AI coding setup; it broke in mostly by trying leaked passwords on FortiGate VPNs and open Remote Desktop (SOCRadar via Crypto Briefing, Oct 30).
  • CISA says attackers are now exploiting 'Node2Shell' (CVE-2026-111497), a critical bug in Google's Brotli compression library; businesses running affected servers should patch and rebuild container images (BleepingComputer, Oct 28).
  • A critical Linux kernel bug (CVE-2026-98432) can give remote attackers full control of some servers; Ubuntu and Red Hat fixes are still rolling out, with no in-the-wild attacks reported yet (BleepingComputer, Oct 30).
  • Old copies of Popcorn Time / Time4Popcorn left a background updater on millions of PCs and Macs that attackers could have hijacked; Malwarebytes says uninstall it (Malwarebytes, Oct 26).
  • Update Microsoft Authenticator on Android: versions before 6.2606.4246 could leak sign-in tokens to other apps, and Microsoft shipped out-of-band Windows updates to distrust compromised eMudhra certificates (SANS Internet Storm Center, Oct 28).
