Hackers used GL.iNet's cloud remote-management service to install malware on home and travel routers and KVM devices

High
GL.iNet GoodCloud compromise (backdoors / reverse shells)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

GL.iNet makes popular small travel and home routers, plus little gadgets that let you remote-control a computer. Criminals found a hole in the company's own cloud management service and used it to put hidden remote-access software on some of these devices. The hole is now closed, but devices already infected stay infected until you update them. If you own one, install the new security update for your exact model today.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Install the latest stable security firmware for your exact router or KVM model from GL.iNet's official security notice, even if your device looks fine (your settings can be kept).
  • Look in the device's process list for the text 'crond -i /tmp/.log'. If it's there, treat the device as infected and update right away.
  • If you're unsure, can't check, or your model isn't listed, contact GL.iNet support instead of guessing — and consider reinstalling firmware without keeping old settings.
  • Change the router's admin password and Wi-Fi password after updating, and turn off remote/cloud management if you don't use it.
  • Don't plug a possibly infected KVM into an important computer until it's updated.

On Nov 20, 2026 GL.iNet confirmed an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability in its GoodCloud remote-management service to install malicious software on some routers and KVM devices. The cloud-side fix blocks new infections but does not clean devices already compromised. GL.iNet's first indicator was a running process containing 'crond -i /tmp/.log'; users and independent researchers also reported custom malware, root-level reverse shells and a persistent older backdoor, and noted the vendor's check may miss some infections. A third-party consultancy reported suspicious activity on 152 of 183 routers it manages (including 24 with reverse shells), though some may predate this incident. GL.iNet released security firmware (e.g. 4.11.1+ for GL-MT3000/MT6000/MG1300; KVM 1.10.3+ for RM1/RM10) that removes the identified malware without a factory reset, and is still investigating whether computers behind infected KVMs were reached.

Platforms: GL.iNet routers (e.g. Beryl, Flint, Slate families) and GL.iNet RM-series KVM devices
How it spreads: Remote exploitation of the vendor's GoodCloud cloud-management service; no user action needed to be infected
Source: BleepingComputer

BADBOX 3.0: cheap Android TV boxes were infected before they left the factory — and rented out your home internet

Med
BADBOX 3.0 / PXYEDGE proxy network
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

Some very cheap, no-name streaming TV boxes and Android devices come with hidden malware already installed. It secretly plays video ads you can't see, and it also lets strangers route their internet traffic through your home connection. You may notice nothing at all. If you own an off-brand streaming box, consider replacing it with a certified one.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Check whether your TV box or tablet is a 'Play Protect certified' Android TV device from a known brand; off-brand AOSP boxes with no certification are the risk.
  • Replace unknown-brand streaming boxes with a mainstream device (Roku, Apple TV, Fire TV, Google TV/Chromecast) from a reputable store.
  • Don't sideload apps onto cheap boxes or use 'free everything' streaming apps.
  • Put IoT/TV devices on your router's guest Wi-Fi so they're separated from your computers and phones.
  • Watch for unexplained slow internet or data usage from the TV box — a sign it may be relaying traffic.

HUMAN Security's Satori team disclosed and disrupted BADBOX 3.0, the largest ad-fraud operation by bid volume ever publicly disclosed: roughly 40 billion fake bid requests per day at its peak (over 4% of observed connected-TV traffic, more than three times the previous record). It runs on low-cost, uncertified Android Open Source Project (AOSP) devices — not Play Protect certified Android TV — that ship with backdoors which pull fraud modules from an actively maintained catalog. Hidden-window video ads impersonate legitimate streaming apps, and the same devices are enrolled in 11 residential proxy networks, including PXYEDGE (successor to IpMoYu, ~200,000 advertised nodes). Parts of the operation used infrastructure disrupted when Google took down the XCPE set-top-box botnet.

Platforms: Low-cost, uncertified Android (AOSP) TV boxes, tablets and phones
How it spreads: Pre-installed at manufacture/distribution; backdoor fetches fraud and proxy modules
Source: BleepingComputer

Bazuka: a new Android trojan that screenshots your home screen and shows it as a fake overlay while it steals logins

High
Bazuka (Android fraud framework)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

Bazuka is an Android banking-theft program. It can take a picture of your home or lock screen and show it on top, so you think nothing is happening while it quietly records what you type and steals text-message codes. Criminals also use an AI helper to pick the richest victims. It spreads through lures like adult-content apps — never install apps from links or outside the official store.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Install apps only from Google Play, and be skeptical even there of apps with few reviews, adult/'free premium' themes, or lots of permission requests.
  • Never grant an app Accessibility access or turn on Developer options / Wireless Debugging because an app asks you to.
  • Keep Google Play Protect on and run a scan if your phone suddenly changes settings by itself.
  • Use an authenticator app or passkeys instead of SMS codes for banking where possible.
  • If you suspect infection, call your bank from the number on your card and uninstall suspicious apps.

ThreatFabric (Nov 18, 2026) found Bazuka in late October while investigating an NFC-relay banking trojan. It's a full framework: dropper/downloader, a core, and 23 modules covering overlays, keylogging, 2FA/SMS interception, remote control and crypto-address swapping. A novel 'optics' overlay screenshots the home/lock screen and displays it on command to hide malicious activity. The core grants itself permissions via Accessibility and via ADB shell commands (enabling Wireless Debugging), and the operator panel integrates local or API-hosted LLMs to score and prioritize victims. Distribution relies on 'traffers' using lures such as adult content; the dropper requests minimal permissions, potentially to pass Google Play review.

Platforms: Android
How it spreads: Lure apps (e.g. adult-content themes) delivered by 'traffers'; may try to reach Google Play with a low-permission dropper
Source: The Hacker News (citing ThreatFabric)

BlackNevas ransomware group wiped a company's VMware servers and backups using stolen admin logins

High
BlackNevas (ESXi wiper/ransomware)
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

In one 10-day attack, criminals logged in with stolen administrator passwords and destroyed a company's virtual servers and the backups meant to rescue them. They didn't need a software bug — just a password taken from another computer. The lesson for any business: keep backups separate and protected, and use different admin accounts for different systems.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • Ask your IT provider whether backups are stored offline or in a separate account that normal admin logins can't reach.
  • Use separate admin accounts for servers, virtualization and backups — never one shared password.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for every admin and remote-access login.
  • Make sure management consoles (vCenter, backup servers) aren't reachable from the open internet.
  • Test restoring from backup at least once a quarter.

Securelist (Nov 13, 2026; covered by Cybersecurity News Nov 16) detailed an intrusion attributed to BlackNevas that destroyed four ESXi hosts, every VM, two vCenter appliances and the backup infrastructure over ten days. No hypervisor vulnerability was exploited: credentials stolen from outside the virtualization environment opened vCenter. Attackers deployed MeshAgent and a persistent Ligolo-ng tunnel disguised as a VMware service, abused vCenter Host Profiles to enable SSH on ESXi hosts (~11 minutes), disabled antivirus and stopped/encrypted backup systems, then ran a wiping payload that overwrote roughly the first 2 KB of virtual-disk files.

Platforms: VMware ESXi, vCenter, backup servers
How it spreads: Stolen valid credentials; abuse of legitimate vCenter management features
Source: Cybersecurity News (citing Securelist)

Chinese state-linked hackers used a VMware vCenter zero-day (CVE-2026-123890); Broadcom issued emergency patches Nov 19

High
Armory Castle (UNC5221) — BONESAW, SAWSNAKE, SLIMSAVAGE
Tap to read the full storyTap to close
In plain English

VMware vCenter is the control panel many businesses use to run their servers. A spy group found a way to walk past its login screen and quietly take over, and they'd been doing it since September. A fix is now out. If your business or your IT provider runs VMware, make sure this update is installed right away.

🛡 How to stay safe
  • If you or your IT provider run VMware vCenter, install the fixed versions listed in Broadcom's advisory VMSA-2026-0009 right now.
  • Make sure vCenter isn't reachable from the internet; limit access with firewall rules (Broadcom says there is no workaround).
  • Also patch other remote-support and edge appliances (like BeyondTrust Remote Support) that attackers used to get in.
  • After patching, ask your IT provider to review admin accounts and look for new SSO administrators.

Broadcom's VMSA-2026-0009 (Nov 19, 2026) fixes a critical VMware Directory Service authentication bypass in vCenter Server (CVE-2026-123890) plus four other flaws in SDDC Manager and VMware Tools for Windows, with no official workaround. Mandiant/GTIG attribute exploitation since early September to Armory Castle (formerly UNC5221), which first compromised BeyondTrust Remote Support appliances, deployed SLIMSAVAGE web shells on Linux systems, then used a custom Go exploit tool, BONESAW, to create an SSO administrator and steal the directory database, and SAWSNAKE to forge SAML tokens (Golden SAML), bypassing MFA. Additional backdoors included CAGEMATCH, BEARBONES and LAZYBONES.

Platforms: VMware vCenter Server, SDDC Manager, VMware Tools for Windows
How it spreads: Unauthenticated network access to vCenter; lateral movement from compromised remote-support appliances
Source: BleepingComputer

Seller-impersonation fraud has more than doubled — now with cloned voices at the closing table

AI scam
In plain English

A new American Land Title Association study found 59% of title firms faced a fake-seller attempt last year, up from 28% in 2024. Scammers now use AI-cloned voices on spoofed phone numbers that look like they come from a lender, title officer or agent, then pressure buyers to wire closing money early. One West Michigan couple wired funds before a closing disclosure was even issued.

How to spot it: Any last-minute change to wiring details, a rush to send funds early, or a 'seller' or 'agent' who never wants to talk on a known phone line — stop and call your lender or title company at a number you already have.
Source: Realty Today (citing ALTA's 2026 Seller Impersonation Fraud Study) · 2026-11-19

AI makes fake online stores and 'missed delivery' texts look real this Black Friday

AI scam
In plain English

Malwarebytes says AI now makes fake storefronts, product photos and reviews look professional, and it can write personal-sounding messages that mention your name. More than one in four consumers surveyed had already hit an AI-related shopping scam, and Amazon is the most impersonated brand. Expect fake delivery notices asking for a small fee or address confirmation.

How to spot it: Too-good prices, a countdown timer, or a delivery text with a link — skip the link and go straight to the retailer's or carrier's official site or app.
Source: Malwarebytes Labs · 2026-11-17

FTC: a familiar voice on the phone is no longer proof — AI can clone it from a short clip

AI scam
In plain English

Consumer coverage of the FTC's latest warning explains that scammers can copy a real person's voice from a short clip posted online, then call claiming to be a hurt or arrested relative or a boss who needs money now. They usually ask for gift cards, wire transfers or crypto because those are hard to recover.

How to spot it: Urgency plus secrecy plus an unusual payment method — hang up and call the person back at a number you already know, or check with another family member.
Source: Syracuse.com / Cleveland.com (citing FTC guidance) · 2026-11-22
